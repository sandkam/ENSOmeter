What is it?

Every few years the equatorial Pacific swings between a warm phase (El Niño) and a cool phase (La Niña), with neutral in between. These swings shift rainfall, storm tracks and temperatures worldwide, and El Niño years tend to raise global mean temperature.

  • El Niño: weakened trade winds, warm water spreads east; wetter in Peru/Ecuador and southern USA, drier in Indonesia, Australia and southern Africa.
  • La Niña: stronger trade winds, cold upwelling in the east; the reverse pattern, and often more active Atlantic hurricane seasons.
  • Strength (ONI): weak 0.5–0.9, moderate 1.0–1.4, strong 1.5–1.9, very strong ≥ 2.0 (same for negative values).

Methodology

  1. Region: Niño 3.4 box, 5°N–5°S, 170°W–120°W in the central Pacific.
  2. Anomaly: the SST minus the climatological average for that calendar period. NOAA uses 30-year base periods (ERSSTv5), updated every 5 years and centered on each date; weekly OISST data use 1991–2020.
  3. ONI (Oceanic Niño Index): the 3-month running mean of monthly Niño 3.4 anomalies (e.g. DJF, JFM, FMA...).
  4. Episode definition: NOAA declares El Niño / La Niña when ONI is ≥ +0.5 / ≤ −0.5 °C for at least 5 consecutive overlapping seasons (and the atmosphere responds).
  5. "Live" value on this page: each day a GitHub Action downloads NOAA CPC's weekly OISST Niño 3.4 anomalies, averages them per month, and takes the 3-month running mean. The last months are partial and may be revised. The official ONI (blue line) lags by about a month.

Note: NOAA CPC has been moving toward a relative ONI (RONI), which subtracts the tropics-wide warming trend. This page shows the traditional ONI.
